# Validation Reflection — tgarrio1

Aidan and I each reviewed the other's ten projects, giving us 100 comparisons across five fields. We agreed on 93 before discussion and all 100 after resolving the seven disagreements. ActivityPattern had the lowest initial agreement at 85%, followed by HasRecovered and RecentThemes at 90%. WhyRecovered and Currentstatus matched in every case. For recovery reasons, we compared the meaning of our answers rather than treating different wording as a disagreement.

Some long gaps followed work that had already shifted toward maintenance. Movsim's last commit before its 17-month gap updated libraries for Java 11. In aglm, feature work was followed by small fixes and demo checks before the long pause. Those changes could reflect a project becoming stable, but the commit messages do not prove why development paused. Recovery often involved compatibility or package maintenance. Aglm returned with CRAN check fixes, vtreat resumed with rebuilding and checking the package, and Movsim restarted with Dependabot updates followed by human contributions.

The recovery disagreements helped me separate resumed activity from the amount of activity. I counted vtreat as recovered because maintenance commits followed its longest gap, even though the project later became Inactive. Aidan accepted that interpretation because the coding guide does not require a minimum number of commits. Vole showed another problem: its apparent 67-month gap came from an old author timestamp. After correcting the gap, we agreed that the later dependency work counted as recovery. These cases showed why a large gap or low commit count does not automatically mean a project has ended.

For activity patterns, checking the official repository and the assignment cutoff mattered. MiDaS had later fork activity in WoC, but its official repository had stopped and was archived before the cutoff. For HIVE, I initially preferred irregular because of its earlier bursts. Aidan rechecked September 30, 2025 and found 11 months without commits, compared with a longest earlier gap of two months, with no pull requests after #9. I accepted stopped. Our shared rule was to use stopped when a project was Inactive at the cutoff and either had an explicit end signal or had been silent longer than its own longest earlier gap. This was our way of applying the category consistently, rather than a threshold stated in the coding guide.

The theme disagreements came from commits that could fit more than one category. We counted bot-authored changes as Automated bot contributions and human dependency updates or merges as Dependency updates. For pyaneti, I had treated added examples as features, but the changes only added usage inputs and data, so I accepted Documentation updates. The main lesson for me was to check the dates, commit authors and changed files before choosing a label. Writing down the rules we used made the final answers more consistent across both sets of projects.
